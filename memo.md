# Memo: Reducing TTC subway delay minutes

**To:** Subway Operations leadership (illustrative)
**From:** Reezwan-Us Sami
**Data:** TTC Subway Delay Data, City of Toronto Open Data, Jan 2023 – Oct 2025, Lines 1, 2 and 4

## Bottom line

The subway lost **3,261 hours** of train delay over 34 months. That time is concentrated in a few places:
- **Causes:** customer-related incidents caused **48.7%** of delay minutes, and **25 of 123** delay codes account for **80%**.
- **Severity:** a small number of long incidents carry a large share of the total.

Focusing on these areas would recover more time than spreading effort evenly.

## What the data shows

1. **Customer incidents dominate.** The top customer-related causes are:

   | Cause | Delay minutes |
   |---|---|
   | Disorderly patrons | 23,827 |
   | Unauthorized people at track level | 14,741 |
   | Ill or injured customers (aid refused) | 10,893 |
   | Ill or injured customers (transported) | 10,720 |

2. **Long delays are rare but costly.** Delays of 30 minutes or more are only **2.5%** of delaying incidents but **19.9%** of delay minutes. "Priority One" incidents, where a train is in contact with a person, have a median delay of 75 minutes.
3. **False alarms cost real time.** Passenger alarms, emergency alarms and equipment calls where "no trouble was found" cost **14,689 minutes (7.5%)**.
4. **Vehicle issues centre on door monitoring.** Door-monitoring (OPTO) faults are the top vehicle cause at **11,370 minutes** across 2,022 incidents.
5. **Location matters.** Ten stations account for **27.7%** of delay minutes, led by Eglinton, St George, Kennedy, Kipling and Bloor. These are mostly terminals and interchanges.
6. **Trend.** Delay minutes for January–October rose from 53,294 in 2023 to 61,659 in 2024, then fell **5.8%** to 58,106 in 2025.
7. **Coding gaps.** About **14%** of delay minutes are logged under vague "other" codes (Security Other, Transportation Other, Miscellaneous Other).

## Recommendations

1. **Deploy staff where and when customer incidents peak.** Station staff and special constables at the top ten stations during the afternoon peak (4–6 pm, the highest-delay hours) address the largest category.
2. **Reduce "no trouble found" alarm time.** Set a target time to reset a train after an alarm where nothing is wrong. Back it with a standard verify-and-release procedure and rider messaging on when to use the alarm.
3. **Write a playbook for long incidents.** Pre-plan shuttle buses and recovery steps for track-level intrusions and Priority One events, and track the time to restore service as a KPI.
4. **Target door-monitoring reliability** in the vehicle maintenance program.
5. **Tighten incident coding.** Retire or split the "other" codes so the 14% of unexplained minutes can be managed.

## How I would measure success

Track these monthly:
- Delay minutes per category.
- Share of delay minutes from incidents of 30+ minutes.
- "No trouble found" minutes.
- Share of minutes under "other" codes.

Compare each against the same months of the previous year to control for seasonality.

## Caveats

- Figures are not adjusted for ridership or service hours.
- Cause categories are a judgement-based grouping of TTC codes.
- Recommendations are illustrative; I have not costed them.
